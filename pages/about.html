About

A molecular geneticist working on healthy aging.

{{WAVE}}

I study why age is the greatest risk factor for chronic disease, and how that risk can be reduced. My central idea is that aging stems from a progressive loss of metabolic flexibility, the capacity of cells, tissues and whole organisms to respond to changes in nutrient supply, and that restoring this “homeodynamic” capacity can promote healthy aging.

My path

From flies and worms to public health.

As a graduate student with Linda Partridge at University College London, I described the first late-onset dietary intervention to reverse mortality trajectories in fruit flies, and showed that the balance of nutrients, not calories, drives the longevity effect of dietary restriction.

As a postdoctoral fellow with Andrew Dillin at the Salk Institute, I identified CRTC coactivators as direct longevity targets of the energy sensor AMPK.

Since 2011 my lab at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health has shown how the nervous system coordinates nutrient-sensing signals to control aging across the body, discovered that RNA splicing factors causally regulate longevity and explain why individuals respond differently to the same intervention, and established that organelles (mitochondria and, most recently, peroxisomes) are how nutrient sensors govern metabolic aging.

The next step is precision geroscience: finding biomarkers that predict who will respond to which intervention, and moving these mechanisms into mammalian systems and human cohorts. As Director of the Harvard Chan Healthy Aging Initiative I focus on the inequities in healthy aging that make this a public-health emergency rather than a luxury problem.

Before graduate school I worked in journalism. During my postdoc I wrote as an accredited freelance science writer (National Association of Science Writers), wrote paper synopses for PLOS Biology, and wrote press releases for the Salk Institute’s communications office. Explaining the science clearly still matters a great deal to me.

Full (about 150 words)

William Mair is Professor of Molecular Metabolism at the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and Director of the Harvard Chan Healthy Aging Initiative. A molecular geneticist, he studies how nutrient and energy sensing shape the rate of aging and why age is the greatest risk factor for chronic disease. His lab discovered that RNA splicing factors causally regulate longevity, defined how the nervous system coordinates AMPK and mTORC1 signaling to control aging across tissues, and showed that organelle plasticity (mitochondrial and peroxisomal) underlies the loss of metabolic flexibility with age. Trained at University College London (Ph.D., with Linda Partridge) and the Salk Institute (with Andrew Dillin), he is an elected member of the Academy of Geroscience, a former permanent member of the NIH CMAD study section, co-founding director of the MBL Biology of Aging course, and organizer of the Cold Spring Harbor Mechanisms of Aging meeting. He is also Chief Scientific Advisor to The WNDR Lab.

01Research overview

I am a molecular geneticist who studies why age is the greatest risk factor for chronic disease, and how that risk can be reduced. My central hypothesis is that aging and age-related disease stem from a progressive loss of metabolic flexibility, the capacity of cells, tissues and whole organisms to respond acutely and appropriately to changes in nutrient supply, and that restoring this "homeodynamic" capacity can promote healthy aging.

As a graduate student with Linda Partridge I described the first late-onset dietary intervention to reverse mortality trajectories in Drosophila and showed that macronutrient balance, not calories, drives the longevity effect of dietary restriction. As a postdoc with Andrew Dillin I identified CRTC coactivators as direct longevity targets of the energy sensor AMPK. My own group at Harvard has since defined how AMPK and mTORC1 integrate in the nervous system to regulate systemic aging cell non-autonomously; discovered causal roles for RNA splicing factors in longevity and in individual variation in response to interventions; and established organelle plasticity, mitochondrial network dynamics, ER-mitochondrial calcium signaling and, most recently, peroxisomal function, as the effector arm through which nutrient sensors govern metabolic aging. Along the way we have built widely used genetic tools in C. elegans for single-copy knock-in, tissue-specific ribosome profiling and acute protein degradation.

The next phase is precision geroscience: defining biomarkers that predict who will respond to which intervention, and translating these mechanisms into mammalian systems and human cohorts. As Director of the Harvard Chan Healthy Aging Initiative I work to integrate the biology of aging with public health, with a particular focus on the inequities in healthy aging that make this a public-health emergency rather than a luxury problem.
